## 1. What the NASA article says

Dates: the article writes plain calendar dates ("May 20", "May 14 and 20, 2024"). It **does not say UTC, Earth date or sol**. The photojournal page gives the sol for the camera images: "on May 20, 2024, the 4,190th Martian day, or sol, of the mission."

**The flare and how NASA got its class**

> "The biggest event occurred on May 20 with a solar flare later estimated to be an X12 — X-class solar flares are the strongest of several types — based on data from the Solar Orbiter spacecraft, a joint mission between ESA (European Space Agency) and NASA."

> "The flare sent out X-rays and gamma rays toward the Red Planet, while a subsequent coronal mass ejection launched charged particles. Moving at the speed of light, the X-rays and gamma rays from the flare arrived first, while the charged particles trailed slightly behind, reaching Mars in just tens of minutes."

**Curiosity RAD**

> "If astronauts had been standing next to NASA's Curiosity Mars rover at the time, they would have received a radiation dose of 8,100 micrograys — equivalent to 30 chest X-rays. While not deadly, it was the biggest surge measured by Curiosity's Radiation Assessment Detector, or RAD, since the rover landed 12 years ago."

- The article does not say over what time span the 8,100 micrograys was received.
- SwRI adds a comparison with normal levels, as a quote from the RAD lead: "While not deadly, it was the biggest surge measured by RAD so far, producing a spike of radiation eight times higher than normal." (SwRI Techbytes)
- PIA26302: "Curiosity's Radiation Assessment Detector (RAD) measured a sharp increase in radiation at this time – the biggest radiation surge the mission has seen since landing in 2012."

**How long the elevated radiation lasted: NOT STATED.** None of the four sources gives a duration for the high radiation on the surface. (Compare 2017, where the source said "The high readings lasted more than two days.")

**Curiosity cameras**

> "During the May 20 event, so much energy from the storm struck the surface that black-and-white images from Curiosity's navigation cameras danced with “snow” — white streaks and specks caused by charged particles hitting the cameras."

PIA26302: "The particles do not damage the detector."

**MAVEN**

> "The purple color in this video shows auroras on Mars' nightside as detected by the ultraviolet instrument aboard NASA's MAVEN orbiter between May 14 and 20, 2024."

> "“This was the largest solar energetic particle event that MAVEN has ever seen,” said MAVEN Space Weather Lead, Christina Lee ... “There have been several solar events in past weeks, so we were seeing wave after wave of particles hitting Mars.”"

Heliophysics article (2025): "As energetic particles from the Sun struck the Martian atmosphere, NASA's MAVEN (Mars Atmosphere and Volatile Evolution) orbiter watched auroras engulf the Red Planet from May 14 to 20."

Why the aurora covers the whole planet (NASA article): "Mars lost its internally generated magnetic field in the ancient past, so there's no protection from the barrage of energetic particles. When charged particles hit the Martian atmosphere, it results in auroras that engulf the entire planet."

**Mars Odyssey**

> "Similarly, the star camera NASA's 2001 Mars Odyssey orbiter uses for orientation was inundated with energy from solar particles, momentarily going out. (Odyssey has other ways to orient itself, and recovered the camera within an hour.)"

The heliophysics article words this differently: "causing the camera to cut out for almost an hour."

**Astronauts**

- The 8,100 micrograys / 30 chest X-rays quote above, plus "While not deadly".
- RAD lead Don Hassler: "Cliffsides or lava tubes would provide additional shielding for an astronaut from such an event. In Mars orbit or deep space, the dose rate would be significantly more."
- One sentence on the page has words missing in the source text itself: "...astronauts, who could use on the Martian landscape for protection." Do not quote it.

**Warning from Earth**

> "The unfolding space weather was closely tracked by analysts at the Moon to Mars Space Weather Analysis Office at NASA's Goddard Space Flight Center in Greenbelt, Maryland, which flagged the possibility of incoming charged particles following the coronal mass ejection."

## 3. Is the 20 May 2024 flare in DONKI?

**No.** The fetched response holds **no flare record with a peakTime on 2024-05-20**, and no X-class flare after 15 May. The nearest records are an M2.5 on 19 May and C4.8 / C6.6 / M1.9 on 21 May. This fits SPEC 9.7's expectation: NASA says the X12 class was "later estimated ... based on data from the Solar Orbiter spacecraft". Every DONKI record here comes from GOES, near Earth.

I also checked DONKI SEP for 8 to 25 May 2024 (fetched to scratch only, not committed, per SPEC 9.2). It holds no particle event on 20 May. All its records come from near-Earth or STEREO A instruments.

Under the project's rule, 20 May therefore gets `flare: "none"` with a `notes` line, even though it is the chapter's main event. See the open questions.

Why 20 May is the storm day:

- The NASA article dates the biggest event "May 20". The particles reached Mars "in just tens of minutes", so the flare day and the radiation day are the same day. In 2017 they were a day apart.
- PIA26302 dates the RAD surge to the same moment as the 20 May images ("RAD ... measured a sharp increase in radiation at this time").

## 6. Open questions for a human

1. **Date range.** Is 11 to 24 May (storm on day 10) acceptable? This drops the 8 to 10 May X-flares and the 10 May Earth storm. Those are Earth events and say nothing about Mars (SPEC 9.2).
2. **Storm day without a DONKI flare.** On 20 May the rule gives `flare: "none"`, the weather tile shows no flare, and there is no blackout on 21 May, although NASA reports an X12. Options: keep the rule and explain it in a `notes` line and the Real/Simplified box, or add a second source type for flare class. The second option is a spec change.
3. **Duration.** No source gives how long the surface radiation stayed high. Should someone look for a RAD paper or data (e.g. the RAD team's 2024 or 2025 publications, or the Planetary Data System) before setting days 11 and 12 above 0?
4. **14 to 19 May.** MAVEN saw auroras and "wave after wave of particles hitting Mars" in this window. That was in orbit and the upper atmosphere, not on the surface. Under SPEC 9.1 these should not become surface radiation levels. Do we agree they stay at 0?
5. **Which calendar.** The article gives "May 20" without a time zone. The game shows "one sol as one Earth date". 20 May 2024 = sol 4190 (PIA26302). Is that enough?
6. **Unit of the 8,100 micrograys.** The article does not say over what period it was received. Cards should keep NASA's comparison ("30 chest X-rays") and not invent a rate.
7. **Second source for the register.** The NASA article (nasa.gov, Jun 10, 2024) would be a new register row, e.g. `curiosity-rad-2024`, group `additional`, kind `reading`. Optional extra rows: PIA26302 and the heliophysics article.
8. **"Region 13664".** DONKI uses NOAA's five-digit number. NOAA often writes this region as "3664", and DONKI's own note says "AR 3664". Which form should the card use?
